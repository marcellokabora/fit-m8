Finding someone who actually shows up, matches your skill level, and wants to play the same
sport as you can feel harder than the workout itself. Here's what actually works.

## 1. Be specific about your level

"Intermediate" means something different to everyone. Instead of guessing, describe what you
can actually do — how long you've played, what format you prefer, and what a good session
looks like to you. The more specific you are, the fewer awkward first sessions you'll have.

![Two people playing tennis on an outdoor court](/blog/finding-your-sports-partner/beachtennis.jpg)

## 2. Set your format, not just your sport

Casual jogging on a Sunday and training for a 10k are both "running," but they're completely
different partners to look for. Filtering by format (casual, competitive, training for an
event) upfront saves everyone time.

## 3. Look nearby, not just "in the city"

A partner 40 minutes away rarely becomes a regular one. Prioritize people close enough that
meeting up doesn't require a special trip — consistency beats novelty.

## 4. Give it more than one session

The first session is often just logistics — figuring out pace, communication style, and
whether your schedules actually line up. Give a new partner at least two or three sessions
before deciding it's not a match.

![Friends playing beach volleyball at sunset](/blog/finding-your-sports-partner/beachvolley.jpg)

## 5. Say what you're looking for, out loud

Some people want a training partner, others want a friend to play with, and some are open to
more. Being upfront about which one you want avoids the awkward mismatch later — and it's the
single biggest reason matches on FIT-M8 stick.
